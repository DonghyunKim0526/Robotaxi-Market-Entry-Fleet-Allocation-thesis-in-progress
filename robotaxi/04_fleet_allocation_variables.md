# Robotaxi Fleet-Allocation Variables

This document summarizes the variables needed to decide how many robotaxis should be allocated to each feasible city or service zone.

## Scope

\[
\text{Demand prediction}
\rightarrow
\text{City feasibility filtering}
\rightarrow
\text{Fleet allocation}
\]

The question addressed here is:

> Given uncertain demand, limited fleet capacity, and operational constraints, how many vehicles should be allocated to each city or service zone?

---

# 1. *Control of Robotic Mobility-on-Demand Systems: A Queueing-Theoretical Perspective*

**Authors:** Rick Zhang and Marco Pavone  
**Publication:** *International Journal of Robotics Research*  
**Paper link:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.4391)  
**Evidence level:** **Direct AMoD optimization evidence on fleet sizing and rebalancing**

The study models an autonomous mobility-on-demand system as a closed queueing network. Vehicles serve passenger trips and then rebalance without passengers to correct spatial demand imbalances.

## Fleet-allocation variables

| Fleet-allocation variable | Role in the paper | Expected effect on required fleet | Potential data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passenger arrival rate by station or zone | Measures customer demand entering each zone | Higher arrival rates increase local vehicle requirements | Robotaxi or TNC trip requests |
| Origin–destination probability | Describes where passengers travel from each origin zone | Unbalanced OD flows increase rebalancing needs | Trip-level origins and destinations |
| Travel time between zones | Determines how long vehicles remain occupied | Longer travel time reduces trips per vehicle | Road travel-time matrix |
| Vehicle availability by zone | Probability that a passenger finds an available vehicle | Lower availability increases passenger loss | Fleet telemetry |
| Passenger loss or unmet demand | Requests not served because no vehicle is available | Higher service targets require more vehicles | Rejected requests |
| Rebalancing flow | Empty vehicles moved between zones | Corrects imbalance but increases empty travel | Fleet telemetry or model output |
| Total fleet size | Total number of vehicles in the system | More vehicles increase availability but also fixed and idle costs | Operator fleet records |

## Other findings

- Spatially asymmetric demand prevents the vehicle distribution from remaining balanced through passenger trips alone.
- Allocating vehicles only in proportion to local demand is insufficient.
- Fleet sizing must account for **vehicle outflows and inflows created by OD patterns**.
- The Manhattan case study estimated that approximately 8,000 robotic vehicles could serve the modeled taxi demand, but this number is specific to the study’s data and assumptions.

## Paper conclusion

> The key fleet-allocation variables are zone-level demand arrival rates, OD flows, travel times, vehicle availability, passenger loss, and empty-vehicle rebalancing. Spatial imbalance in demand makes rebalancing an essential component of fleet sizing.

# 2. *Congestion-aware Routing and Rebalancing of Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems in Mixed Traffic*

**Authors:** Salomón Wollenstein-Betech et al.  
**Paper link:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04335)  
**Evidence level:** **Direct AMoD research integrating congestion, routing, and rebalancing**

This study models passenger flows and empty rebalancing flows together with road congestion and endogenous travel time.

## Fleet-allocation variables

| Variable | Role in the paper | Expected effect | Potential official or operational data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Road-link capacity | Maximum traffic flow that can be handled by each link | Lower capacity increases congestion and travel time | FHWA or local DOT |
| Background private-vehicle traffic | Traffic not generated by the AMoD fleet | Increases congestion and vehicle cycle time | FHWA HPMS or NPMRDS |
| Passenger vehicle flow | Flow of occupied AMoD vehicles | Uses fleet time and road capacity | Fleet records |
| Empty rebalancing flow | Flow of unoccupied AMoD vehicles | Improves availability but increases congestion and cost | Fleet records |
| Link travel time | Travel time as a function of network flow | Lower vehicle productivity when congestion rises | NPMRDS or operator GPS |
| Routing choice | Routes used by passenger and empty vehicles | Determines total travel time and congestion exposure | Optimization output |

## Other findings

- Empty rebalancing improves fleet availability but also adds traffic.
- At high demand levels, passenger and empty flows can worsen congestion.
- Congestion should therefore be modeled as a fleet-productivity and rebalancing-cost factor, not only as a city-entry penalty.

## Paper conclusion

> Traffic congestion, link capacity, route choice, background traffic, and empty rebalancing flows jointly determine the effective productivity of an autonomous fleet. Fleet allocation should therefore be coupled with routing rather than calculated from trip counts alone.

# 3. *Electric Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand: Jointly Optimal Vehicle Design and Fleet Operation*

**Authors:** Fabio Paparella, Theo Hofman, and Mauro Salazar  
**Paper link:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13012)  
**Evidence level:** **Direct E-AMoD evidence integrating fleet size, battery capacity, charging, and cost**

The study jointly optimizes electric vehicle design and fleet operation, showing that fleet size, battery capacity, charging, fixed cost, and operational efficiency are interdependent.

## Fleet-allocation variables

| Variable | Role in the paper | Expected effect on fleet size | Potential measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of vehicles | Core fleet-design decision | Increases service capacity but also fixed cost | Decision variable |
| Battery capacity | Determines driving range and charging pattern | Smaller batteries reduce vehicle cost but may increase charging frequency | Vehicle specification |
| State of charge | Determines whether a vehicle can serve a trip or must charge | Low-SOC vehicles are temporarily unavailable for service | Vehicle telemetry |
| Energy use by trip | Energy required for each OD movement | Longer or congested trips increase charging needs | Distance × energy efficiency |
| Charging opportunity and duration | Time a vehicle is removed from passenger service | Longer charging time increases nominal fleet requirement | Charger and vehicle data |
| Vehicle fixed cost | Cost of purchasing and holding another vehicle | Discourages over-allocation | Operator accounting |
| Battery degradation cost | Cost caused by driving and charging cycles | Influences operating and charging schedules | Battery model |
| Trip revenue | Economic value of served demand | Can prioritize more profitable demand | Fare data |
| Unserved-trip opportunity cost | Value lost when demand is not served | Higher shortage penalties increase optimal fleet size | Rejected requests × margin |

## Other findings

- Fleet size and battery capacity should not be optimized independently.
- There is a trade-off between using more vehicles with smaller batteries and fewer vehicles with larger batteries.
- Charging infrastructure and charging duration directly constrain effective fleet capacity.

## Paper conclusion

> For an electric robotaxi system, fleet size cannot be determined independently of battery capacity, energy consumption, state of charge, charging time, fixed vehicle costs, and operational revenues.

# Integrated Fleet-Allocation Formulation

## Decision variables

\[
x_{i,t}
=
\text{available vehicles in zone }i\text{ at time }t
\]

\[
r_{ij,t}
=
\text{empty vehicles rebalanced from zone }i\text{ to zone }j
\]

\[
q_{i,t}
=
\text{vehicles charging in zone }i\text{ at time }t
\]

## Uncertain demand

\[
D_{i,t}^{\omega}
=
\text{ride demand in zone }i\text{ at time }t\text{ under scenario }\omega
\]

## Objective

\[
\min
\mathbb E_{\omega}
\left[
\sum_{i,t}
c_i^{u}u_{i,t}^{\omega}
+
c_i^{o}o_{i,t}^{\omega}
+
\sum_{i,j,t}c_{ij}^{r}r_{ij,t}^{\omega}
+
\sum_{i,t}c_i^{ch}q_{i,t}^{\omega}
\right]
\]

where:

- \(u_{i,t}^{\omega}\): unmet demand;
- \(o_{i,t}^{\omega}\): idle or excess vehicles;
- \(r_{ij,t}^{\omega}\): empty rebalancing;
- \(q_{i,t}^{\omega}\): charging vehicles.

## Basic constraints

### Fleet limit

\[
\sum_i x_{i,0}\le Q
\]

### Demand balance

\[
\text{served}_{i,t}^{\omega}
+
u_{i,t}^{\omega}
=
D_{i,t}^{\omega}
\]

### Vehicle-flow balance

\[
x_{i,t+1}^{\omega}
=
x_{i,t}^{\omega}
-\text{passenger departures}_{i,t}^{\omega}
+\text{passenger arrivals}_{i,t}^{\omega}
-\sum_j r_{ij,t}^{\omega}
+\sum_j r_{ji,t}^{\omega}
-q_{i,t}^{\omega}
+\text{returned from charging}_{i,t}^{\omega}
\]

### Charging capacity

\[
q_{i,t}^{\omega}\le C_i^{charger}
\]

### Weather and ODD availability

\[
\text{served}_{i,t}^{\omega}
\le
a_{i,t}^{\omega}x_{i,t}^{\omega}
\]

# Connection to Expected Over- and Under-Allocation Costs

Let \(R_{i,t}\) be the actual future number of vehicles required and \(x_{i,t}\) the allocated fleet.

## Under-allocation cost

\[
c_{i,t}^{under}(R_{i,t}-x_{i,t})^+
\]

Possible components:

- rejected trips;
- passenger waiting;
- lost fare revenue;
- service-level violations;
- customer abandonment;
- emergency repositioning.

## Over-allocation cost

\[
c_{i,t}^{over}(x_{i,t}-R_{i,t})^+
\]

Possible components:

- idle vehicle time;
- depreciation;
- depot and parking cost;
- insurance;
- charging standby;
- opportunity cost of not allocating the vehicle elsewhere.

The basic objective is:

\[
\min_x
\mathbb E
\left[
\sum_{i,t}
c_{i,t}^{under}(R_{i,t}-x_{i,t})^+
+
c_{i,t}^{over}(x_{i,t}-R_{i,t})^+
\right]
\]
