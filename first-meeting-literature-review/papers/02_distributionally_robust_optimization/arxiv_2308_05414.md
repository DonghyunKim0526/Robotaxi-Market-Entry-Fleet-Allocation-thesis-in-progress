# Unifying Distributionally Robust Optimization via Optimal Transport Theory

## One-Sentence Summary

This paper provides a theoretical framework that unifies several distributionally robust optimization models, especially Wasserstein DRO and phi-divergence DRO, using optimal transport theory.

## 1. Big Picture

This paper is more theoretical than the applied MR-DRO and charging station papers.

Its main purpose is to explain how different DRO frameworks are connected.

When reading DRO literature, one confusing issue is that different papers define ambiguity sets in different ways.

Some papers use:

- KL divergence;
- chi-square divergence;
- total variation;
- Wasserstein distance;
- moment constraints;
- optimal transport distances.

At first, these may look like separate approaches. This paper shows that some of these approaches can be understood under a broader optimal transport framework.

The main value of the paper for me is conceptual:

> Different ambiguity sets represent different assumptions about how the empirical or nominal distribution might be wrong.

## 2. Why This Paper Matters

DRO depends heavily on the ambiguity set.

The ambiguity set is not just a technical detail. It determines:

- what types of distributional errors are protected against;
- how conservative the solution becomes;
- whether the problem is computationally tractable;
- how the model behaves out of sample.

Therefore, understanding the difference between ambiguity sets is important.

This paper helps organize the DRO landscape.

## 3. Main Types of DRO Ambiguity Sets

Two important ambiguity set families are:

1. phi-divergence ambiguity sets;
2. Wasserstein ambiguity sets.

They are both used in DRO, but they protect against different kinds of distributional uncertainty.

## 4. Phi-Divergence DRO

Phi-divergence DRO measures how much one distribution differs from another through a divergence function.

Examples include:

- KL divergence;
- chi-square divergence;
- Burg entropy;
- total variation-like divergences.

The key intuition is:

> Phi-divergence DRO often changes probability weights assigned to existing scenarios.

Suppose we have empirical samples:

    ξ1, ξ2, ξ3, ..., ξn

A phi-divergence ambiguity set may allow the probabilities of these samples to change.

For example, harmful scenarios may receive more probability mass in the worst case.

But the scenario locations themselves usually remain fixed.

So the interpretation is:

    The observed outcomes are trusted as possible outcomes,
    but their probabilities may be wrong.

This is useful when the sample support is credible but probability weights are uncertain.

## 5. Wasserstein DRO

Wasserstein DRO is different.

It allows probability mass to move in the uncertainty space.

The observed samples are not fixed completely.

The interpretation is:

    The observed samples may not perfectly represent future realizations.
    Future outcomes may be near the observed samples but not exactly the same.

This is useful when:

- sample values may shift;
- historical data may not cover future values exactly;
- the distribution may move spatially or structurally;
- data are biased;
- the future environment may differ from the past.

This is why Wasserstein DRO is attractive for logistics, demand, and resource allocation problems.

## 6. Key Difference: Reweighting versus Moving Scenarios

The most useful distinction for me is:

    Phi-divergence DRO:
    The model mainly reweights existing scenarios.

    Wasserstein DRO:
    The model can move scenarios in the uncertainty space.

This distinction helped me understand why the MR-DRO papers use Wasserstein ambiguity sets.

In multi-source information problems, the reference distribution is constructed from imperfect predictions. It is not safe to assume that only probabilities are wrong. The scenario values themselves may also be biased or shifted.

Therefore, Wasserstein DRO is natural.

## 7. Optimal Transport Perspective

Optimal transport studies how one probability distribution can be transformed into another by moving probability mass.

The Wasserstein distance is one of the main distances in optimal transport.

This paper uses optimal transport theory to show that multiple DRO models can be expressed in a common framework.

In a simplified interpretation:

- classic Wasserstein DRO allows movement in the outcome space;
- phi-divergence DRO can be interpreted through a different type of transport or lifted formulation;
- broader transport-based ambiguity sets can capture both movement of outcomes and changes in probability weights.

The technical details are advanced, but the conceptual message is important:

> DRO ambiguity sets can be understood as different ways of allowing the empirical distribution to be perturbed.

## 8. Why This Matters for Modeling Choice

When applying DRO, one should ask:

> What kind of error do I believe exists in the data or nominal distribution?

If I believe the historical scenarios are correct but their probabilities are uncertain, phi-divergence DRO may be appropriate.

If I believe future outcomes may shift away from historical samples, Wasserstein DRO may be appropriate.

If both probability weights and outcome values may be uncertain, a more general optimal transport framework may be needed.

This is not just theoretical. It affects decisions.

## 9. Connection to Multi-Sourced Data

In multi-source optimization, each source may provide biased or noisy predictions.

For example:

- one source may systematically overpredict demand;
- another may underpredict in certain regions;
- sources may be accurate in different contexts;
- source errors may be correlated.

In this setting, the empirical or fused distribution may be wrong in both probability weights and scenario values.

Wasserstein DRO protects against this by allowing distributions near the fused distribution.

The unifying optimal transport paper helps explain why this approach is reasonable.

## 10. Connection to the Trust-Aided MR-DRO Paper

In the trust-aided resource allocation paper, multiple source predictions are fused into a reference distribution. Then a Wasserstein ambiguity set is built around it.

This means the model says:

1. The fused distribution is our best current estimate.
2. But it may still be wrong.
3. Therefore, we protect against nearby distributions.

This is exactly the kind of reasoning that the optimal transport DRO framework supports.

## 11. Connection to Unknown-Trust MR-DRO

In the unknown-trust MR-DRO paper, the trust-weighted empirical distribution is constructed from source predictions and historical errors.

Again, this distribution is not treated as the true distribution.

It is only the center of a Wasserstein ambiguity set.

This distinction is important:

> Trust helps construct a better center. Wasserstein DRO protects against remaining distributional error.

So trust and DRO play different roles.

## 12. My Understanding

My current understanding is:

> The paper teaches that different DRO ambiguity sets are not arbitrary. Each one encodes a different belief about how the empirical distribution may be wrong.

Phi-divergence DRO says:

    The sample outcomes are probably right, but their probabilities may be wrong.

Wasserstein DRO says:

    The sample outcomes themselves may also be shifted or incomplete.

Optimal transport provides a broader language for expressing these forms of uncertainty.

## 13. Practical Interpretation for Logistics

In logistics or military resource allocation, the type of distributional error matters.

### Case 1: Probability Error

Suppose historical demand levels are representative, but the probability of high-demand days is uncertain.

Phi-divergence DRO may be reasonable.

### Case 2: Scenario Shift

Suppose future demand may occur in new locations or at different magnitudes than historical samples.

Wasserstein DRO may be more appropriate.

### Case 3: Multi-Source Bias

Suppose drone, satellite, social media, and official reports all provide biased but useful signals.

A Wasserstein or transport-based ambiguity set may help protect against biased reference distributions.
