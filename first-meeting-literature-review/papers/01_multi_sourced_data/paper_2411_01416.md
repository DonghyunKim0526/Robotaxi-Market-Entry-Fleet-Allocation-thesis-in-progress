# Sequential Charging Station Location Optimization under Uncertain Charging Behavior and User Growth

## One-Sentence Summary

This paper studies where and when to build electric vehicle charging stations over multiple years when future demand growth is uncertain and users choose stations probabilistically based on distance, station type, and home charging options.

## 1. Big Picture

This paper is about sequential infrastructure planning.

The planner does not build all charging stations at once. Instead, the planner decides where and what type of charging stations to build over several years.

The difficulty is that future EV charging demand is uncertain, and users do not simply choose the nearest station deterministically. Their choices depend on station type, distance, station attractiveness, and the availability of home charging.

The paper combines:

- multi-stage stochastic programming;
- EV demand growth uncertainty;
- multinomial logit user choice model;
- charging station location decisions;
- station type decisions;
- efficient reformulation methods.

## 2. Correct Classification

This paper is best understood as a multi-stage stochastic programming paper.

It is not primarily a DRO paper because it does not use a Wasserstein ball or worst-case distribution.

It is not an ILO or decision-focused learning paper because it does not learn a prediction model end-to-end through decision loss.

If connected to contextual optimization taxonomy, it is closest to a sequential learning and optimization structure:

    generate demand scenarios
    → solve stochastic optimization problem
    → evaluate planning decisions

## 3. Research Questions

The paper asks:

1. When future EV demand growth is uncertain, where should charging stations be built over time?
2. Should the planner build small or large charging stations at each location?
3. How does probabilistic user choice affect station location decisions?
4. How much value does multi-stage planning add compared with two-stage planning?
5. How can the difficult nonlinear formulation caused by the MNL model be solved efficiently?

## 4. Decision Variables

The key decision variable is:

    x^t_jk = 1 if station type k exists at location j by year t

This means the model decides both:

- where to build;
- what type or size of station to build.

The station type index `k` can represent small and large stations.

A newly built station can be represented as:

    x^t_jk - x^(t-1)_jk

The model assumes that once a station is built, it remains available in future years.

## 5. User Choice Model: MNL

Users choose among charging options probabilistically.

The probability that user node `i` chooses station location `j` of type `k` is modeled using a multinomial logit structure.

The attractiveness of a station depends on:

- station type;
- location attractiveness;
- distance from user node;
- home charging option;
- availability of other stations.

The distance coefficient is usually negative, meaning users are less likely to choose stations farther away.

This is important because opening a station in one location affects not only nearby users but also competition among all stations.

## 6. Why a Location with No Visible Demand May Be Selected

A candidate location does not need to have a demand node exactly on top of it.

A location such as L9 can be selected even if it appears to have little direct demand nearby because the model considers:

- surrounding demand nodes;
- travel distance;
- station attractiveness;
- station type;
- budget constraints;
- competition with other stations;
- future demand scenarios;
- marginal contribution to total expected revenue.

Therefore, the selected location is not based only on the demand shown at the exact candidate site.

This was an important clarification from our discussion. The model is not simply picking points with visible demand. It is optimizing the whole network of demand, stations, user choice, and future growth.

## 7. Two-Stage versus Multi-Stage

### 7.1 Two-Stage Model

The two-stage model fixes a plan for the whole planning horizon early.

It is simpler and computationally easier.

However, it cannot fully adapt to future demand realizations.

### 7.2 Multi-Stage Model

The multi-stage model allows decisions to adapt over time as demand information is revealed.

The planner makes a decision, observes demand evolution, then makes later decisions based on that observed history.

The key idea is not that the planner knows the future. Rather, the model enforces nonanticipativity:

> If two scenarios have the same history up to a given time, the decisions up to that time must be the same.

This captures realistic sequential decision-making.

## 8. Nested Max-Expectation Interpretation

The nested max-expectation formulation represents the timeline of decisions and uncertainty:

    decision
    → demand uncertainty realized
    → next decision
    → next demand uncertainty realized
    → next decision

This structure allows future decisions to depend on information observed along the way.

The model is valuable when future demand paths can diverge significantly across regions.

In our discussion, the key point was that multi-stage does not mean the planner magically knows future demand. It means the planner can revise future decisions after observing earlier demand realizations.

## 9. Solution Approach

The problem is difficult for two main reasons.

First, future demand growth is uncertain and must be approximated by a finite scenario tree.

Second, the MNL choice probabilities create nonlinear fractional expressions.

The paper solves this by:

1. approximating future demand growth with a finite scenario tree;
2. assigning each scenario a demand trajectory;
3. imposing nonanticipativity constraints;
4. reformulating the MNL-related nonlinear expressions;
5. using second-order conic or subgradient inequality methods;
6. solving the problem efficiently with SGI-based methods.

The main computational contribution is to handle the MNL fractional integer structure efficiently.

## 10. Computational Results

The paper uses a case study based on Trois-Rivières, Québec.

Key setting:

- 4-year planning horizon;
- 58 user demand nodes;
- 10 candidate locations;
- 2 station types;
- 81 scenarios.

The results show that multi-stage planning can outperform two-stage planning.

Important findings:

- in the baseline setting, the multi-stage model gives about 2.26% higher objective value than the two-stage model;
- when a new urban sub-center appears, the gap increases to about 9.40%;
- when demand growth variability increases, the value of multi-stage planning increases;
- when users are less sensitive to distance, the gap between two-stage and multi-stage planning becomes smaller;
- when demand is spatially dispersed, multi-stage planning becomes more valuable.

## 11. Why the Objective Gap Matters

A 2% objective improvement may look small, but in infrastructure planning, practical significance depends on scale.

If the planning budget or revenue is large, even a few percent improvement can be meaningful.

However, the multi-stage model is much more computationally expensive.

Therefore, one important question is:

> When is the additional performance gain of multi-stage planning large enough to justify the computational cost?

This question is especially relevant for practical decision-making.

## 12. Limitations

### 12.1 Gaussian Demand Growth

The paper assumes demand growth follows a Gaussian distribution.

Real EV demand growth may involve:

- policy shocks;
- market disruptions;
- heavy tails;
- spatial correlation;
- nonlinear adoption patterns.

### 12.2 Scenario Tree Sensitivity

The solution may depend on:

- number of scenarios;
- random seed;
- scenario generation method;
- scenario reduction method.

### 12.3 MNL Parameter Uncertainty

The MNL parameters are treated as known.

In practice, parameters such as the distance coefficient and attractiveness terms are estimated from data and have uncertainty.

### 12.4 Rolling-Horizon Comparison

The paper compares two-stage and multi-stage models, but a realistic alternative is rolling-horizon re-optimization.

In practice, planners often update the plan every year based on newly observed demand.

A useful extension would compare:

- two-stage planning;
- multi-stage planning;
- rolling-horizon re-optimization.

### 12.5 Computational Cost

The multi-stage model is much more expensive than the two-stage model.

The value of multi-stage planning should therefore be compared against its computational burden.
